\abstract{
Precipitation phase partitioning is a leading source of uncertainty in
mountain hydrology, where rain--snow misclassification degrades
streamflow forecasting and water-resource management. Conventional
methods relying on sparse station networks and near-surface thermodynamic
thresholds are limited near freezing, where rain and snow distributions
overlap. Our framework assimilates crowdsourced phase reports from the
Mountain Rain or Snow (MRoS) network with station meteorology and
satellite-derived probability of liquid precipitation, interpolates
every source to an hourly 1-km grid, and trains a calibrated XGBoost
machine learning model on held-out MRoS labels across California's Sierra Nevada and
Colorado's Rocky Mountains from October 2022 to May 2026. We benchmark
against nine standard partitioning methods with bootstrapping. 
The model reached 91.2\% accuracy in California and 91.4\% in
Colorado, and 85.6\% and 82.7\% near freezing against 69.1\% and 74.3\%
for the best benchmark in each domain, with a test AUROC of 0.965 in
both. Its accuracy gain over that benchmark was 11.1 percentage points
in California (95\% CI 9.0--13.4) and 16.5 points near freezing
(12.2--21.1). Though Colorado's accuracy gain was marginal (3.6 points,
0.2--6.9), its macro F1 gain was meaningful (0.096, 0.042--0.152). Rather
than classify mixed precipitation as a distinct phase, we additionally compare
observer-reported mixed events against the model's region of
uncertainty, which flags where mixed phase is likely and captures 
about a third of individual events. Ablation studies trace model improvements to the 
assimilated predictors, above all the crowdsourced surfaces.
}

\statement
Predicting whether mountain precipitation falls as rain or snow is
essential for water-supply and flood forecasting. However, the process is challenging
and prone to errors near freezing temperatures, where sparse weather stations and simple
temperature-based partitioning methods struggle to correctly classify precipitation phase. 
We show that combining crowdsourced phase reports from volunteer observers 
with station, satellite, and terrain data on a common 1-km grid substantially 
improves rain--snow prediction across California and Colorado mountain ranges, 
especially near freezing.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% Paragraph 1 -- Why phase matters: mountain hydrology and its forecast chain
Determining whether cold-season precipitation falls as rain or snow is a
critical yet challenging area of research in mountain hydrology.
Seasonal mountain snowpack supplies water to more than one-sixth of the
global population \citep{barnett2005}, and in the western United States
53\% of total runoff originates as snowmelt, rising to roughly 70\% in
mountainous terrain \citep{li2017snowrunoff}. That snowpack functions as
natural storage, accumulating through the winter and releasing gradually
during melt. In contrast, rainfall can turn into runoff within hours, avoid 
capture for storage, and contribute to potentially hazardous streamflow conditions. 
Depending on its precipitating phase, a single
storm can therefore recharge water supply or trigger flooding. An error 
in phase classification further propagates downstream
into snowpack estimates, runoff timing, flood magnitude, and the
forecasts issued to mountain communities and backcountry users
\citep{harpold2017rain, jennings2019sensitivity}. Warming temperatures
have meanwhile shifted more cold-season precipitation from snow to rain and
increased rain-on-snow flood risk across western North America
\citep{musselman2018}, which has compoundded uncertainty in water supply
planning and hazards forecasting.

% Paragraph 2a -- Why it is hard: observational limits and mixed phase
Phase remains difficult to classify accurately, especially near freezing.
Precipitation gauges in mountainous terrain are geographically sparse
relative to the heterogeneous conditions they are intended to record.
Few stations report phase at all, and those that do often sit where
cold-air pooling, wind exposure, and steep elevation gradients decouple
recorded estimates from true conditions in their surroundings
\citep{lundquist2008rain, minder2011, wayand2016}. Phase also resists a
clean definition. Near freezing, hydrometeors may arrive as graupel, ice
pellets, freezing rain, or wet mixtures that share a range of surface air
temperatures (Section~\ref{sec:mix}). This physical ambiguity makes
classifying mixed precipitation separately from rain or snow both a
predictive challenge and a significant source of error.

% Paragraph 2b -- Why it is hard: methods and the predictor ceiling
Conventional partitioning methods struggle as well, since most land
surface and hydrologic models partition rain from snow with a
single near-surface air-temperature threshold or a smooth transition
around one \citep{ding2014, dai2008}. This approach measurably alters simulated
accumulation and melt \citep{jennings2019sensitivity} without translating
to regions where transitional phase temperatures can vary by more than
\degC{4} \citep{jennings2018spatial}. Wet-bulb temperature supplements
phase classification by capturing the cooling of hydrometeors falling
through unsaturated air \citep{harder2013, sims2015, wang2019}, but no
surface measurement resolves melting and refreezing aloft, so errors tend to
concentrate near freezing \citep{feiccabrino2015}. Increasing model
complexity does not necessarily fix the issue either, since
\citet{jennings2025} found that fine-tuning machine learning models 
marginally improves on simple partitioning rules given near-surface
meteorology alone.

% Paragraph 3 -- The observational gap and crowdsourcing
Addressing this ceiling in predictive accuracy requires new model
inputs. Ground-based phase reports at transition-zone elevations can
supplement synoptic weather stations, which are sparse and typically
biased toward lower elevations and airports. Crowdsourcing helps fill
atmospheric observations where instruments are absent
\citep{muller2015}, and the Mountain Rain or Snow (MRoS) project does so
by collecting timestamped, geolocated visual reports from citizen
scientist volunteers across mountain regions \citep{arienzo2021}. Such
reports have helped characterize rain--snow and mixed conditions and have
been used to benchmark phase partitioning methods
\citep{jennings2023mros,jennings2025}. Their value as a
predictor---assimilated alongside station and satellite data into a
spatially continuous, probabilistic field---has yet to be explored.

% Paragraph 4 -- This study: research questions and contributions
This study explores how to overcome such limitations in phase prediction. 
Three research questions frame our work:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Can assimilating a wider variety of predictors (i.e., interpolated
  crowdsourced phase observations, interpolated station meteorology, 
  and satellite-derived probability of liquid precipitation (pLP)) 
  improve rain--snow accuracy beyond what near-surface thermodynamics alone can supply?
  \item Does this data assimilation approach enhance predictive phase accuracy 
  near freezing where conventional threshold partitioning methods still struggle?
  \item Can mixed precipitation, which is usually discarded or
  forced into an ill-defined class, exhibit meaningful signal in a model's
  region of predictive uncertainty?
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The framework we propose combines four complementary sources
of data: crowdsourced phase reports from MRoS, hourly station meteorology, 
satellite-derived pLP \citep{huffman2020imerg}, and topographic elevation
(Section~\ref{sec:data}). The station meteorology and crowdsourced
observations are interpolated into their own surfaces, which are then
assimilated with resampled pLP and elevation onto a common hourly
1-km grid.

We train a binary XGBoost classifier \citep{chen2016} on held-out rain--snow MRoS phase
labels and evaluate it in two climatologically distinct ranges, which differ
in precipitation pattern, terrain, and observer density. We then
benchmark against the standard partitioning methods surveyed by
\citet{jennings2025} using clustered resampling to quantify
significance and produce confidence intervals.

\section{Study domains and period}
\label{sec:domains}

Two mountain domains are processed independently through an identical
pipeline: the California Sierra Nevada Mountains near Lake Tahoe (projected 
coordinate system in UTM Zone 11N, EPSG:26911) and the Colorado Rocky Mountains 
(projected coordinate system in UTM Zone 13N, EPSG:32613).
Each is bounded by a polygon over mountain terrain, and the study period
spans 1 October 2022 to 1 May 2026, excluding June, July and August as 
summer non-observation season. All datasets are harmonized to a
common 1-km analysis grid whose coordinates and elevations derive from a
digital elevation model (DEM). The domains contrast usefully
(Figure~\ref{fig:domain}): California has much wider covariation of air
temperature with elevation, atmospheric-river-driven storms, and a
comparatively balanced observation mix, whereas Colorado's continental
snow climate is strongly snow-dominant with sparser rain.

\subsection{Crowdsourced phase observations}
MRoS citizen scientists submit timestamped, geolocated reports of
precipitation phase (rain, snow, or mixed) during storm events
\citep{arienzo2021}. Volunteers who have opted into storm text alerts,
issued when forecast surface temperatures fall in the rain--snow
uncertainty range of roughly $-2$ to \degC{10}, open a browser-based app
during precipitation and record what phase is currently falling. The
device supplies geolocation and timestamp automatically. The protocol
operationalizes the phase definitions of Section~\ref{sec:intro}:
observers report what is falling rather than what has accumulated,
classify graupel as snow and freezing rain as rain, exclude hail and true
sleet, and reserve the mixed label for genuinely ambiguous or rapidly
transitioning precipitation. Neither temperature nor intensity is entered 
by the observer, so each record reduces to a geotagged, timestamped phase label.

Each submission is processed with the \texttt{rainOrSnowTools} package
\citep{rainorsnowtools}, which tags it with USGS 3DEP elevation and with
modeled surface meteorology from stations within a radius of one degree
of latitude and longitude (requiring at least five stations) after
standardized range and consistency checks. Quality assurance follows the procedures established
for the network \citep{arienzo2021, jennings2023mros}: only real-time
reports are retained, since ancillary fields are matched to each report's
exact time and location, and observations lacking nearby station support
or failing physical plausibility checks are removed. After this, the
rain--snow observation set comprises 15{,}337 observations in
California (10{,}735 train / 2{,}301 validation / 2{,}301 test; 64\% snow)
and 3{,}492 in Colorado (2{,}444 / 524 / 524; 82\% snow).
Observer-reported mixed events constitute a further 12.2\% of California
test observations (320 of 2{,}621) and 10.9\% of Colorado's (64 of 588);
they are held aside from binary training and used for uncertainty-band
evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:mix}).

\subsection{Station meteorology}
Hourly surface observations are collected from four public networks
(HADS, LCD, WCC, and MADIS; Table~\ref{tab:predictors}) via the
\texttt{rainOrSnowTools} access layer \citep{rainorsnowtools}. The retained variables are air
temperature, dewpoint, and relative humidity, from which wet-bulb
temperature is derived during assimilation (Section~\ref{sec:assim}).
These stations provide the thermodynamic environment that is
interpolated to the analysis grid.

\subsection{Satellite probability of liquid precipitation}
Half-hourly GPM IMERG probability-of-liquid-precipitation (pLP) fields
\citep{huffman2020imerg} are retrieved from NASA GES DISC via OPeNDAP,
subset server-side to each domain, and merged across the 48
half-hourly timesteps into daily gridded tables. Derived from passive
microwave and infrared retrievals, pLP is an independent estimate of
rain or snow that carries information about the precipitating column
which near-surface measurements cannot supply.

\subsection{Reanalysis climate fields}
\label{sec:reanalysis}
We explored daily PRISM rasters \citep{daly2008} as a spatially
complete backdrop to the sparse station network after resampling its available
variables from 800~m to the 1-km grid. PRISM regridded cleanly in space,
however its daily resolution is too coarse for an hourly phase product. 
Expanding it to hourly required imposing a diurnal temperature
curve, and that synthetic sub-daily structure did not improve validation
skill over the station-interpolated fields while risking bias near
freezing, so we excluded it.
Coarser global reanalyses (ERA5, MERRA-2) were similarly rejected
because their 30--50-km resolutions cannot resolve the kilometer-scale
elevational gradients that control phase here.

\subsection{Terrain}
Raw 10-m DEMs from the USGS 3DEP / National Map are clipped to each
domain, reprojected to the regional UTM CRS, and resampled to 1 km by
average pooling. The resulting DEM defines the analysis grid and supplies
the elevation field used for lapse-rate detrending, kriging drift, and as
a model predictor.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

The methodology divides into two pipelines run identically per domain
(Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}). A data-preparation pipeline 
fuses station meteorology, external gridded products, and MRoS reports, then interpolates them onto
the common analysis grid, producing gridded predictor fields and
leakage-safe MRoS phase-probability predictors. A modeling pipeline trains and calibrates the binary
classifier, derives a mixed-phase uncertainty band, and evaluates the
results through bootstrapping, ablation, and benchmarking.

\subsection{Data assimilation and quality control}
\label{sec:assim}
An assimilation stage cleans the station record, derives consistent
meteorology, and harmonizes every source to the DEM grid before
interpolation. Missing thermodynamic variables are reconstructed from
physical relationships. Saturation vapor pressure follows
Magnus--Tetens, and dewpoint and relative humidity are interchanged to
fill whichever is missing. Wet-bulb temperature, the most physically
informative single predictor of phase, is estimated with Stull's formula
\citep{stull2011}, refined by an iterative psychrometric solution and
constrained between dewpoint and air temperature. Where a station
reports air temperature without co-located humidity, dewpoint and
relative humidity are gap-filled at the station using
lapse-rate-adjusted inverse-distance weighting (IDW) from neighboring
stations, following the \texttt{model\_meteo} procedure of
\texttt{rainOrSnowTools} \citep{rainorsnowtools}.

Station observations then pass through quality control: duplicates are
dropped, hard physical cutoffs remove impossible readings, a per-station
interquartile-range filter removes outliers, and cross-variable checks reconcile
temperature with humidity. Remaining data points are aggregated to one record per
station per hour (the last observation within the hour). 
A dynamic lapse rate is estimated at each timestep from
the station temperature--elevation relationship, letting the vertical
structure vary hour to hour, and DEM elevation is appended to every point
dataset so all sources share an elevation reference. IMERG is brought to
the hourly 1-km grid by bilinear resampling, and all retained fields are
synchronized on a common UTC axis and written to hourly NetCDF stacks.

\subsection{Spatial interpolation}
\label{sec:interp}
All point observations are interpolated to the 1-km analysis grid by
kriging, the best linear unbiased predictor for a spatial field
\citep{cressie1993, goovaerts1997}, with a spherical model fitted to the
empirical semivariogram. Because hourly observations are sparse, we pool
the empirical semivariogram across time before fitting, which stabilizes
the covariance estimate during thinly observed hours.

Each variable is kriged in the form matched to its physical nature. Air and
dewpoint temperature carry a strong elevation trend, so they are
detrended against station elevation with the hourly lapse rate,
interpolated by ordinary kriging on the residuals, and retrended with DEM
elevations, a regression-kriging that preserves physical vertical
structure \citep{hengl2007}. Relative humidity is interpolated by
universal kriging with DEM elevation as an external drift term
\citep{wackernagel2003} to reflect terrain dependence, even though it 
is used only to derive dewpoint and wet-bulb temperature rather 
than enter the model as a predictor.

Precipitation phase is nominal, so encoding snow, mix, or rain ordinally and
interpolating would impose artificial arithmetic (i.e., mix would represent a 
meaningless average of snow and rain). We therefore convert phase to
one-hot indicators and krige each class independently by indicator
kriging \citep{journel1983}, which estimates the conditional probability
of that class at each grid cell. Because the kriging estimator is not
constrained to the unit interval, we clip to non-negative values and
renormalize so the three sum to one, yielding hourly probability
surfaces $p_{\mathrm{snow}}$, $p_{\mathrm{mix}}$, $p_{\mathrm{rain}}$.
Figure~\ref{fig:interpolations} in the appendix shows the full set at one
storm timestamp per domain.

\paragraph{Leakage prevention.}
MRoS observations serve both as training labels and---through the
interpolated phase surfaces---as candidate predictors. Reading an
observation's predictor from a full-grid surface built with that same
observation would let the label leak into the feature. To break the
dependence we compute the MRoS phase predictors by pointwise spatial
leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV): each report is removed from its
hour, and the remaining reports in that hour are re-kriged by indicator
kriging to the held-out location under the same minimum-neighbor
requirement. The resulting triplet is clipped, renormalized and stored
as the leakage-safe predictor, leaving the phase implied by other
observers nearby. Full-grid surfaces
built from all observations are retained separately for mapping and
gridded prediction, never as training features. This mirrors the
leave-one-out logic used for unbiased local estimates in geostatistical
cross-validation \citep{goovaerts1997}.


\subsection{Machine learning model}
\label{sec:ml}
We frame phase prediction as binary classification and train an
XGBoost model \citep{chen2016} with a logistic objective on rain--snow
observations only. Gridded predictor inputs are 
air, dewpoint, and wet-bulb temperature, elevation, IMERG pLP, and the three leakage-safe LOOCV
MRoS phase probabilities. We refer to this full eight-predictor configuration 
as ``MRoS-XGB'' throughout for simplicity. Data are split 70/15/15 into
train/validation/test, stratified on observed phase class with a fixed
random seed, and early stopping is applied on the validation subset (up
to 2{,}000 rounds, patience 50; selected iterations 323 in CA and 513
in CO). The output is the probability of snow, with $p(\mathrm{rain}) = 1 - p(\mathrm{snow})$.

We discarded two alternative architectures along the way: a three-class model of
snow, mix and rain, because mixed precipitation does not form a
separable cluster in feature space (Section~\ref{sec:mix}), and
spatially blocked splits, because blocking left the calibration sets too
small to fit stably. The randomized stratified split adopted instead
preserves the distribution of meteorological states across subsets,
matching the design of \citet{jennings2025}. The cost is
that observations from the same storm may appear in both train and test,
so test performance may be mildly optimistic for a novel season.

\paragraph{Class imbalance.}
Both training sets skew toward snow (64\% in California, 82\% in
Colorado). The MRoS network favors higher-elevation, snowier sites, and
those sites are also colder, drier, and carry higher LOOCV snow
probabilities. The standard remedy to counteract class imbalance is 
frequency-inverse weighting, which scales effective counts
rather than the recall asymmetry itself, but can therefore over- or
under-correct. We instead tune XGBoost's \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight}
against the quantity of interest, training a probe model across a grid
of candidate weights and selecting the value that minimizes the absolute
snow--rain recall gap on the validation set (0.50 for California, 0.15
for the more snow-dominant Colorado). Both fall below one and downweight the majority snow class,
and the resulting recalls balance to within 0.03 and 0.06 respectively
(Section~\ref{sec:skill-results}). Because class weighting shifts the
raw score distribution, we next apply calibration.

\paragraph{Probability calibration.}
\label{par:calibration}
A classifier can rank cases correctly while reporting unverified probabilities, 
and gradient-boosted trees are particularly prone to this
as each boosting round tends to push scores toward 0 and 1, which reflects an
overconfidence that the class weighting above amplifies. Because 
downstream applications need a $p(\mathrm{snow})$ that means what
it reports, and because the uncertainty envelope of
Section~\ref{sec:mix} is an interval in probability space whose
placement is meaningful only if the probabilities are, we calibrate 
the raw model scores to better match observed frequencies.

We fit a post-hoc map from raw score to calibrated
probability, and two features of the problem shape its construction.
First, near \degC{0} wet-bulb the thermodynamic signal is weak and true
probabilities compress toward 0.5, while away from freezing they
approach 0 or 1. One map cannot serve both regimes, so we fit separate
calibrators for near-freezing ($|T_w| \le \degC{2}$, consistent with the
\citealt{sims2015} uncertainty range) and away-from-freezing
observations and route each by its wet-bulb temperature. 
Second, the choice of calibrator matters more than usual for a
well-separated classifier. Isotonic regression, the conventional
non-parametric choice, did not work well here because held-out scores fell outside
the range seen during fitting, and clipping them to that range mapped
62\% of test predictions to exactly 0 or 1. 
We therefore adopt beta calibration \citep{kull2017}, which
is parametric, monotone, and defined on the open unit interval, so it
cannot produce degenerate outputs, and whose three parameters let the map
be asymmetric where the score distribution is. Calibrators are fitted on
final scores over the pooled train and validation rain--snow
observations and applied forward to the untouched test set, with mixed
observations excluded entirely. Calibration adjusts probability
magnitudes only, leaving rank ordering and hard decisions unchanged.

\subsection{Handling mixed phase with an uncertainty envelope}
\label{sec:mix}
Because rain and snow are more physically defined than mix
(Section~\ref{sec:intro}), standard treatments tend to mishandle that
asymmetry. Discarding observer-reported mix, as most partitioning
studies do, throws away the observations closest to the very transition
the model is trying to resolve. Training mix as a third class instead
presumes it is a well-defined category, which it is not. Mixed labels
are only 11 to 12\% of the total, and they overlap both pure phases across
the same near-freezing temperatures rather than occupying a distinct
region of feature space. A trained third class would therefore learn mostly label
noise while degrading the rain--snow boundary, which is what our first
multiclass iteration found (Section~\ref{sec:ml}).

Considering the above, we instead keep the supervised problem binary, and identify 
possible overlapping mix events with the model's region of uncertainty. 
Where that region should sit, however, is not obvious. A fixed threshold 
would seem arbitrary and physically unjustified when the 
meaning of an intermediate score depends on thermodynamic context. 
We therefore let a threshold width vary continuously with wet-bulb
temperature through a Gaussian half-band $h(T_w)$ applied post-hoc to the
calibrated probabilities:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    h(T_w) &= b + a\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{T_w^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),\\
    \text{rain threshold} &= 0.5 - h(T_w),\\
    \text{snow threshold} &= 0.5 + h(T_w),
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:band}
\end{equation}
The band is centered at $T_w = \degC{0}$ and decays as a Gaussian of
width $\sigma$, with $b$ setting the minimum half-width far from
freezing and $a$ the additional widening at \degC{0}
(Figure~\ref{fig:band}). Any observation whose calibrated
$p(\mathrm{snow})$ falls inside the band is flagged as uncertain rather
than assigned a phase. We refer to this as an \emph{uncertainty envelope} to
emphasize that it does not predict a mixed class but delineates, in
probability space, the zone within which the binary model loses
confident discrimination and thus abstains from making a distinct call.
Observer-reported mix events then serve as an independent check on
whether that zone lands where real mixed precipitation occurs.

Two metrics follow from this construction, defined in
Table~\ref{tab:metrics}: the \emph{flag rate} or abstain rate, a
ground-truth independent property of model behavior, and the
\emph{mix-capture rate}, conditional on observer labels. They vary
independently, since a model can flag frequently and still miss real mix
if the envelope is centered wrongly, or flag rarely and capture most of
it if the envelope is well placed.

The three band parameters are optimized on the validation set, which
for this purpose alone includes the observed mixed events. We optimize
a composite of mix capture and confident rain--snow accuracy, subject
to a minimum rain--snow coverage constraint, with $\sigma$ bounded to
physically reasonable transition-zone widths \citep{sims2015,
jennings2025}. The fitted parameters could be tuned but are set to be 
identical in both domains for simplicity ($b = 0.20$, $a = 0.15$, $\sigma = \degC{2.0}$), 
resulting in thresholds at $p < 0.15$ for rain and $p > 0.85$ for snow at \degC{0} wet-bulb and
relaxing them to $0.30$ and $0.70$ far from freezing.

\subsection{Evaluation framework}
\label{sec:eval-framework}
Results are then evaluated in three ways. A feature ablation
(Section~\ref{sec:ablation-methods}) retrains the model with inputs
withheld to establish which data sources are most important, a benchmark
comparison (Section~\ref{sec:bench-methods}) scores it against
operational partitioning methods overall and near freezing, and the
uncertainty envelope is assessed against observer-reported mixed events
(Section~\ref{sec:mix}). Every result is reported with a confidence
interval, and every comparison as a difference with an interval of its
own (Section~\ref{sec:uq}).

Every metric is additionally computed on a \emph{near-freezing subset},
defined throughout as $|T_w| \le \degC{2}$ after the \citet{sims2015}
uncertainty range. This is an observation-level selection in wet-bulb
temperature and should be distinguished from the \degC{0}--\degC{4}
range of 50\% rain--snow air-temperature thresholds reported across
sites by \citet{jennings2018spatial}, which some figures mark for
reference. The two describe a similar physical regime, since
wet-bulb temperature sits below air temperature in the unsaturated
conditions typical of these storms.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
We select a suite of complementary metrics to reflect model performance. 
We generally investigate whether phase is correct on rain as well as on the 
dominant snow class, whether output probabilities can be trusted at
face value, and whether model abstention falls on the right mix cases.
Table~\ref{tab:metrics} accordingly groups the metrics by what they
measure. Accuracy compared against macro F1 diagnoses class-balance
effects, while phase bias compared against accuracy separates aggregate
from event-level correctness (Section~\ref{sec:bench-results}). Because
a committed model call discards the confidence behind it, a second group
scores the probabilities directly. A third scores the mix-related flag
rate, which has no counterpart in the rain--snow benchmarks and is the
only group computed over mixed-phase observations.

\subsubsection{Confidence intervals and significance}
\label{sec:uq}
Because a metric computed on a single test set is only one draw from a
sampling process, we bootstrap the saved test-set predictions
\citep{efron1993}, resample with replacement 5{,}000 times, and
recompute every metric on each copy. Intervals span the 2.5th to
97.5th percentile of the resulting values. Point estimates are computed
once on the full test set rather than as medians over resamples, so
intervals are not necessarily symmetric. 
All intervals are conditional on the trained model and the
realized split, and quantify test-set sampling uncertainty only.

Two departures from the standard procedure are noted. First, MRoS
observations are not independent, since many reports duplicate one
another within a storm-day, so resampling individual rows would credit
the test set with more evidence than it holds and give intervals that
are too narrow. We therefore resample intact clusters, defined as the
observations sharing a 30-km spatial block and a calendar day. Interval
widths are essentially identical across 15-, 30- and 60-km blocks, so
the imposed geometry does not affect the results, although clustering
itself widens intervals by 13 to 28\%. Second, methods are compared
inside each resample rather than by determining whether two separately
computed intervals overlap. Scoring all methods on identical resampled 
rows and recording the difference cancels
any shared variability, for the same reason a paired test outperforms a
two-sample test on the same data. One-sided $p$-values are the fraction
of resamples in which the benchmark matched or exceeded the model.

\subsubsection{Ablation design}
\label{sec:ablation-methods}
Deciding which inputs an operational pipeline should maintain requires
knowing not only which features a model uses but which it needs. We use
SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) \citep{lundberg2020} to identify
which features the fitted model leans on, and then we use those to inform the 
selection of predictors for ablation experiments, which retrain the model 
with subsets of the eight predictors to quantify their individual 
contribution to model skill.

Seven configurations are evaluated per domain, listed with their
retained predictors in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, in three groups: the
full eight-predictor configuration, leave-one-out removals isolating
each assimilated source in turn, and reduced sets testing collective
value (a thermodynamics-only configuration and two reduced ``cores''). All
share identical splits and the same sampled predictor matrix, so
differences are attributable only to the feature configuration, and all
are scored on the metrics of Table~\ref{tab:metrics} with near-freezing
values tracked separately.

\subsubsection{Benchmark methods}
\label{sec:bench-methods}
We next compare against the standard precipitation-phase partitioning
methods surveyed by \citet{jennings2025}, implemented as deterministic
functions of the same gridded predictor fields: static air-temperature
thresholds ($T_a$ = 1.0 and \degC{1.5}), dewpoint thresholds ($T_d$ =
0.0 and \degC{0.5}), and wet-bulb thresholds ($T_w$ = 0.0, 0.5,
and \degC{1.0}). Two logistic methods are also included: the binary
logistic regression of \citet{jennings2018spatial},
\begin{equation}
  p(\mathrm{snow}) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-10.04 + 1.41\,T_a
  + 0.09\,\mathrm{RH})} ,
  \label{eq:logistic}
\end{equation}
where RH is relative humidity, and the same logistic form re-fitted to
each domain's training split,
which isolates the benefit of local recalibration from model form. All
methods are evaluated on the identical held-out test split. Following
\citet{jennings2025}, the primary comparison is binary
(observer-reported mixed excluded), scored with accuracy, macro F1, and
per-phase bias, reported overall and by temperature bin, with the
near-freezing regime reported separately. The threshold and logistic
benchmarks are strictly binary and cannot predict mixed events, so
mixed-phase skill is evaluated separately from the binary comparison.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Reported skill values are point estimates with 95\% confidence
intervals, and comparisons between methods or feature sets are
differences computed on identical resampled data, each carrying an
interval of its own (Section~\ref{sec:uq}). Unless stated otherwise, all
values are held-out test-set values; the few validation-set quantities
are labeled as such. Primary performance results for both domains are
collected in Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap}, per-method benchmark results in
Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}, and per-configuration ablation results in
Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.

\subsection{Overall model skill}
\label{sec:skill-results}
MRoS-XGB performs comparably in the two domains despite their
contrasting snow climates, with a test AUROC of 0.965 in both and an
accuracy of 91.2\% in California and 91.4\% in Colorado
(Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap}; Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} gives the
corresponding confusion matrices). Colorado's intervals are roughly three
times wider than those of California's, reflecting its smaller test set (524
rain--snow observations in Colorado against 2{,}301 in California).

Class weighting balances the two recalls as intended, to within 0.03 in
California and 0.06 in Colorado despite snow shares of 64\% and 82\%
(Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap}). It does so at negligible loss in aggregate
skill, since macro F1 changes by less than 0.01 in both domains, so
correcting the recall asymmetry does not trade against overall
performance.

What remains of the imbalance appears in phase bias, the percentage by
which each phase was predicted more or less often than it was observed.
California shows none that we can detect, since both of its intervals
contain zero. Colorado predicts snow 4.4\% less often than observed and
rain 19.8\% more often, and neither of its intervals contains zero. This
is the cost of the class weighting: downweighting the majority snow
class balances the two recalls, but it does so by shifting the predicted
totals toward rain. The size of that shift is poorly constrained,
because Colorado's test set holds only 96 rain observations, but its
direction is clear.

Beta calibration pulls the raw scores toward the reliability diagonal
in both domains (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}), giving calibrated Brier
scores of 0.070 in California and 0.058 in Colorado. The miscalibration
that remains sits at the extremes of $p(\mathrm{snow})$, where the score
distribution is strongly bimodal and most predictions fall.

\subsection{Skill in the near-freezing regime}
\label{sec:nearfreezing-results}
Accuracy falls near freezing in both domains, as expected. On the
near-freezing subset ($|T_w| \le \degC{2}$; $n = 782$ in California,
$n = 179$ in Colorado), accuracy drops 5.6 and 8.7 percentage points
below each domain's overall figure. AUROC falls much less, from 0.965 to
0.922 and 0.920 (Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap}), so the model continues to
rank snow above rain reliably even where its committed calls are least
accurate. This subset is defined by wet-bulb temperature and is not the
same population as the observations the model declines to call, which is
defined in probability space (Section~\ref{sec:mix-results}); the two
overlap heavily but are scored separately throughout.

Resolving skill into \degC{1} wet-bulb bins shows that the two domains
lose accuracy in structurally different ways
(Figure~\ref{fig:twet}). California's per-phase F1 is non-monotonic in
$T_w$, with a minimum near \degC{1.5}, whereas Colorado's degrades
smoothly as $T_w$ warms, and the breakdown against air temperature
(Figure~\ref{fig:f1-temp}) reproduces both structures. California's
wider covariation of temperature with elevation and its bimodal
distribution of observation elevations (Figure~\ref{fig:domain}) would
plausibly produce several distinct near-freezing regimes where
Colorado's continental climate produces one, although the present sample
cannot separate those contributions.

\subsection{Input-dependent skill}
\label{sec:ablation-results}
Our first research question asks whether the assimilated predictors
contribute skill beyond thermodynamics. We answer it in two steps,
asking first which predictors the fitted model uses and then which it
needs.

\paragraph{Feature attribution.}
SHAP attributions show that the two domains lean on different predictors
(Figure~\ref{fig:shap-wetbulb}). California is
led by the leakage-safe MRoS surfaces and elevation, with the raw
temperatures contributing little, whereas Colorado is led by IMERG pLP
and air temperature. Comparing attribution on the near-freezing subset
with the clear-phase subset, elevation and the MRoS surfaces gain
importance in both domains, and in Colorado the three MRoS surfaces gain
the most of any predictor (ratios of 1.6--1.9). The interpolated MRoS
mix surface is among them in both domains, which is worth distinguishing
carefully. Observer-reported mix is excluded from the training labels
but still enters the model as a predictor, so mixed reports carry usable
signal even though they are never a target.

Some inputs are correlated, which limits what attribution can
establish. Air and wet-bulb temperature correlate at 0.81 in California
and 0.97 in Colorado, the MRoS snow and rain surfaces at $-0.92$ and
$-0.89$, and IMERG pLP with the MRoS snow surface at $-0.75$ in
California. SHAP credit assigned to one member of a correlated group is
therefore not a statement about how much unique information that
predictor supplies, and the ablation addresses this directly. We 
refer to ablation experiments as \emph{configurations} rather than separate models,
since all seven share an identical training procedure, split, and
predictor matrix and differ only in which predictors they retain; the
full eight-predictor configuration is MRoS-XGB itself.
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} gives each configuration's absolute skill and
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation-ci} the paired difference from the full
configuration, which is the skill that configuration would forfeit
without the withheld input.

In California, removing the MRoS surfaces has the single most damaging effect, worth 9.6
percentage points of near-freezing accuracy (6.5--12.7). Dropping
all five non-thermodynamic predictors next costs 15.7 points (12.2--19.4). 
Looking at the paired differences, we notice that the three temperature variables are
largely interchangeable on the overall metrics, but that
interchangeability breaks down at the phase boundary, where wet-bulb
temperature is the only one whose contribution the data resolve (1.7
near-freezing points, 0.4--3.0). Elevation also contributes
measurably on every metric, costing 1.7 points of overall accuracy
(0.8--2.5) when removed. Finally, IMERG pLP contributes specifically near
freezing, worth 1.9 points (0.5--3.3) there, while its effect on 
overall performance stays within noise.

Colorado's test set is smaller, so fewer of its removals resolve at all.
Only three do: dropping all five non-thermodynamic predictors costs 11.2
near-freezing points (3.1--19.5), the MRoS surfaces register on overall
AUROC (0.017, 0.004--0.033), and pLP registers on near-freezing AUROC
(0.017, 0.001--0.036), the same near-freezing role it plays in
California.

Near-freezing AUROC is the one metric on which both domains tell the
same story. It rises from the thermodynamics-only values of 0.796
(0.761--0.830) in California and 0.791 (0.715--0.862) in Colorado to
0.922 and 0.920 under the full configuration, gains of 0.126
(0.096--0.158) and 0.129 (0.066--0.196) that are clear of zero in each.

Resolving the MRoS ablation against temperature

and misses the one that is locally unexpected. The same comparison

snow-side loss considerably, consistent with wet-bulb temperature being
the sharper discriminator of phase.

\paragraph{Phase bias.}
Phase bias separates the benchmarks more sharply than accuracy does.
Near freezing in California, the $T_a = \degC{2.0}$ and
$T_w = \degC{2.0}$ thresholds are set at the same nominal value yet fail
by comparable margins in opposite directions, the former calling snow
76\% less often than it occurred and the latter 74\% more often
(Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}). Wet-bulb temperature sits well below air
temperature in the dry, unsaturated conditions typical of these storms,
so the wet-bulb cutoff admits far more observations to the snow class.
Because these errors partly cancel within a domain, accuracy alone
conceals them.

Only two California methods predict each phase about as often as it
occurred: MRoS-XGB and the \citet{jennings2018spatial} logistic applied
without refitting. The transferred logistic reproduces the observed
totals but is correct on only 72\% of individual events, whereas
MRoS-XGB reproduces them at 91\% accuracy. Predicting the right amount
of snow is not the same as predicting it on the right days, and the
latter is useful for forcing a hydrologic model.
Figure~\ref{fig:tair-bias} resolves these biases against air
temperature for the full benchmark suite, showing that each threshold's
error reverses sign across its own threshold, so a method can appear
accurate in a bin while being badly biased in both phases within it.

\subsection{Uncertainty envelope and mixed precipitation}
\label{sec:mix-results}
The third research question concerns the observations the model declines
to call. Neither accuracy nor the benchmark suite applies here, since no
partitioning method in the comparison considers mixed phase or abstains
from a call. Because the envelope is widest at $T_w = \degC{0}$ by
construction (Equation~\ref{eq:band}), the flag rate peaking there
(Figure~\ref{fig:twet}) reflects the design rather than the data, and
the test is instead whether observer-reported mixed events, withheld
from training, fall inside it (Figures~\ref{fig:band}
and~\ref{fig:mix-capture}).

Overall, the envelope abstains near the rate at which mixed precipitation was
reported; it flags 12.9\% of California test observations against an
observed mix fraction of 12.2\%, and 14.0\% of Colorado's against
10.9\%. The volume of abstention is thus aligned without
having been fitted to that quantity.

Capture of individual events is lower, at 30\% (95\% CI 25--36\%) of
held-out mixed events in California and 33\% (21--45\%) in Colorado. The
misses are not near-misses, since nearly all carry confident
probabilities ($p > 0.8$ or $p < 0.2$), so a wider band would recover
few of them. The domains also differ in where captured events fall
(Figure~\ref{fig:band}): California's span a wide range of temperature
and probability, whereas Colorado's cluster near
$p \approx 0.5$--$0.65$ and $T_w \in [-1, +2]\,^{\circ}$C.

Band width ultimately trades capture against coverage, since a wider band catches
more mixed events but declines to call more of the rain and snow the
model would have classified correctly. The operating point is selected
on the validation set, where it captures 36\% of California's mixed
events, commits to 90\% of its rain and snow, and is correct on 94.4\%
of those calls. Narrowing the far-from-freezing
half-width from 0.20 to 0.10 raises coverage from 90\% to 94\% as
capture falls from 0.36 to 0.20, with Colorado following the same curve
at uniformly lower capture.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The limitation of near-surface thermodynamic predictors}
\citet{jennings2025} found that machine learning barely improves on
threshold rules given near-surface meteorology, and our
thermodynamics-only configuration reproduces that ceiling. MRoS-XGB, through
its addition of satellite pLP and crowdsourced surfaces, surpasses that ceiling. 
Because the ablations highlight the difference in the assimilated
predictors rather than in model architecture, we interpret the ceiling
as a limit of the inputs themselves.

Satellite pLP senses the precipitating column that surface instruments
cannot reach, and crowdsourced reports record the phase that arrived at the
ground. Adding either therefore supplies a new observation type rather than a
better fit to the same predictors. Interpolation is then what makes them
usable, placing every sparse source on a common grid and converting
point reports into a predictor surface. Predictors that describe the
vertical structure of the atmosphere more directly, such as radar or
lidar profiles, would be the next natural addition.

\subsection{The value of crowdsourced observations}
The crowdsourced contribution concentrates at the phase boundary, where
it is worth roughly 2.7 times as much as away from freezing
(Section~\ref{sec:ablation-results}). This is also where an individual
observer is often the only instrument at that elevation and moment, so
these reports record what the conventional station network cannot measure. 
A model of this type can therefore flag reports inconsistent with their 
surroundings, supplying potentially useful quality control for a data 
stream that is otherwise difficult to validate. Which source carries 
that information further depends on the domain. The
crowdsourced surfaces dominate where the observer network is dense, and
satellite pLP registers instead where it is sparse
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\subsection{Model uncertainty and mixed precipitation}
Treating mix as a region of model uncertainty rather than as a class or
discardable dataset (Section~\ref{sec:mix}) keeps the supervised problem binary
and turns the withheld events into an out-of-sample test of model
confidence, which provides evidence of mixed phase. Although the model abstains 
near the observed mix rate in both domains, it captures only about a third of individual
events. However, the test establishes that discarded mixed reports
carry usable signal, both as a predictor surface and as an evaluation
set that most groups working on phase partitioning already hold.

\subsection{Transferable methods}
Two methodological results transfer beyond precipitation phase, and the
first concerns calibration. Isotonic regression, the typical calibrator for classifiers, 
performed worse than its parametric beta calibration alternative. 
Any well-separated, class-imbalanced classifier whose probabilities 
will be used downstream should also be tested with several calibrator types in order 
to select the most appropriate one.

The second finding concerns clustered observations, which describe most
opportunistic environmental data. The cost of correcting for spatial
autocorrelation depends on the stage at which the correction is applied.
Blocking the train--test split consumes training data that a small dataset 
may not be able to spare, whereas resampling clusters 
reshapes only the error bars in a saved prediction table
(Section~\ref{sec:uq}).

\subsection{Relevance, limits, and outlook}
\label{sec:limits}
Calibrated probabilities can replace static thresholds as phase
forcings in hydrologic and land surface models. Because the uncertainty
flag concentrates where phase is genuinely ambiguous, an operational
system can route those hours to a forecaster rather than commit to a
call the model does not support. Not every predictor is needed to do
this. The reduced core of six matches the full eight on every overall
metric, so a group with fewer data sources to maintain could use it,
accepting a measurable loss near freezing.

Whether phase performance gains propagate to streamflow remains untested. 
Because partitioning choice measurably alters simulated accumulation and melt
\citep{jennings2019sensitivity}, a gain of this size at the rain--snow
boundary should register downstream, but demonstrating that it does
requires forcing a hydrologic model with these fields. The contribution
claimed here is therefore to phase classification rather than to
streamflow prediction.

Three further limits apply to the results. The randomized split may
admit mild within-storm leakage, so point estimates may be slightly
optimistic for a novel season, and all intervals are conditional on the
trained model and split. The MRoS network is opportunistic and skewed
toward higher elevations, roads and daylight hours, observer definitions
are inconsistent for mix, and a 1-km cell average cannot capture
sub-grid phase transitions. And with results resting on two domains and
one observation network, transferability remains to be demonstrated.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
We developed a framework that assimilates citizen-science phase
observations, station meteorology, satellite probability of liquid
precipitation, and terrain onto hourly 1-km grids and trains a
calibrated binary classifier with an uncertainty-derived mixed-phase
flag. Across two contrasting mountain domains, the framework
outperformed every standard partitioning benchmark we tested, and by the
widest margin near freezing, where phase is least certain and most
consequential for hydrology.

The ablations trace that advantage to the assimilated predictors rather
than to the choice of model, and the crowdsourced surfaces prove valuable. 
This locates the skill ceiling reported for machine learning
on near-surface meteorology in the type of input observation available rather
than in the architecture applied to it. 
It also gives observer-reported mixed precipitation, which
partitioning studies routinely discard, two uses it has not been put to
before: an interpolated predictor surface and an out-of-sample test of
whether a model's uncertainty falls where phase is genuinely ambiguous.
Two supporting methods, regime-stratified probability calibration and
paired cluster bootstrapping, can further transfer to any problem built on sparse,
spatially clustered observations.

In practice, these calibrated probabilities can replace the static
temperature thresholds that hydrologic and land surface models still
rely on, with the uncertainty flag marking the hours no method resolves
confidently. The framework is decisive where the observer network is
dense and directionally consistent where it is sparse, which leaves two
questions open. Further experimentation within one domain could establish
how far observer density can fall before the crowdsourced contribution
does, and forcing a hydrologic model with these fields could explore
whether improved accuracy at the rain--snow boundary propagates to
simulated snowpack and streamflow.

  score histograms show that most predictions are confident, with the
  band occupying the sparse middle.
  %
  Only pure snow and rain observations enter these diagrams, since calibration is defined against
  a binary outcome; mix is assessed separately in
  Figure~\ref{fig:band}. Brier scores in the panel titles use the same
  subset, raw versus calibrated.
  %
  Departures from the diagonal persist in the outermost bins after
  calibration, for two reasons. Beta calibration is a three-parameter
  monotone map, so it corrects the overall shape of the reliability curve
  but cannot bend to fit a deviation confined to the extremes. And because
  the score distribution is bimodal, a quantile-spaced bin at either end
  spans a very narrow range of predicted probability while collecting a
  large share of the observations, so its mean prediction is pinned near 0
  or 1 while a small number of confidently misclassified observations
  moves its observed frequency vertically. The residual therefore reflects
  cases where the model was confident and wrong, which no calibrator can
  remove, rather than a systematic bias in the map itself.}
